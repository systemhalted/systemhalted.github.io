Right from the moment we are born, we have to learn several things - sucking, crawling, drinking, walking, running, and in general living. And this education continues till we are on our death-bed. We excel at few things and suck at few. And that is what makes us human.

Human? We suck at several things - we cannot drive properly, we do not have patience, we kill each other, we try to harm others, and we all are bloody racists. 

Human civilization exists precisely because we can transmit lessons across generations. Nobody needs to rediscover calculus, germ theory, electricity, constitutional government, or semiconductor physics from scratch. If humans are exceptionally good at accumulating technical knowledge, why are we seemingly much worse at accumulating behavioral and social wisdom? There is a saying, "History repeats itself". Yes, it does, because we bloody humans cannot even learn from our mistakes, how the hell are we suppose to learn from the mistakes of our prior generations? We have to make the mistakes ourselves, and then the same mistakes need to be repeated by our next generation. And the cycle continues. 

What if the next step in human intelligence is not about making individuals smarter, but building a system that prevents humanity from repeatedly forgetting what humanity has already learned?

# Research Notes: Do We Need a Hive Mind?

These references support the central questions behind the topic of the essay:

1. Why do humans fail to learn from mistakes?
2. Why does access to information not guarantee behavioral change?
3. How does knowledge survive across generations?
4. Can groups become more intelligent than individuals?
5. Can collective intelligence also amplify error?
6. Could AI become part of a more effective system of collective memory and reasoning?

# References

## Learning From Failure

1. [Not Learning From Failure--the Greatest Failure of All](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619881133)  
   **Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and Ayelet Fishbach. Psychological Science, 2019. 30(12):1733-1744. DOI: 10.1177/0956797619881133.**

   Across five studies involving 1,674 participants, people learned less from failure feedback than from success feedback even when both conveyed equivalent information. Failure also reduced memory for participants' previous answers. The authors found that failure can threaten the ego, causing people to disengage from the information. Importantly, participants were much more capable of learning from another person's failure, where the threat to their own self-image was reduced.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Failure does not automatically become knowledge. A person can experience an error, receive the information necessary to correct it, and still fail to incorporate that information.

2. [You Think Failure Is Hard? So Is Learning From It](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916211059817)  
   **Lauren Eskreis-Winkler and Ayelet Fishbach. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2022. 17(6):1511-1524. DOI: 10.1177/17456916211059817.**

   This review develops a framework for understanding why failure is difficult to learn from. The authors distinguish between emotional barriers and cognitive barriers. Emotionally, failure threatens the ego and can cause people to disengage. Cognitively, failure often provides indirect information: it tells us that one approach did not work but does not necessarily reveal which alternative will work.

   **Relevance to the essay:** The problem is not simply that humans forget mistakes. Failure itself can interfere with the cognitive process required to extract a lesson from it.

3. [Learning from Errors](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022)  
   **Janet Metcalfe. Annual Review of Psychology, 2017. 68:465-489. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022.**

   This review examines experimental research on error-based learning. Contrary to approaches that try to prevent mistakes during learning, evidence shows that making errors can substantially improve learning when the error is followed by effective corrective feedback. Errors therefore have educational value, but the correction mechanism is critical.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Experience alone is not enough. A mistake becomes useful when a system helps identify it, correct it, and retain the correction.

4. [Mind Your Errors: Evidence for a Neural Mechanism Linking Growth Mind-Set to Adaptive Posterror Adjustments](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611419520)  
   **Jason S. Moser, Hans S. Schroder, Carrie Heeter, Tim P. Moran, and Yu-Hao Lee. Psychological Science, 2011. 22(12):1484-1489. DOI: 10.1177/0956797611419520.**

   Using event-related brain potentials, the researchers found that people with stronger growth-mindset beliefs showed increased neural attention to mistakes. This increased attention predicted better performance following an error. The important difference was not merely recognizing that an error occurred, but continuing to process it.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Two people can make the same mistake but learn different amounts from it because they allocate different levels of attention to the error.

5. [Error-Related Negativity Predicts Reinforcement Learning and Conflict Biases](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2005.06.020)  
   **Michael J. Frank, Brion S. Woroch, and Tim Curran. Neuron, 2005. 47(4):495-501. DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2005.06.020.**

   The study examined error-related negativity, an electrophysiological signal associated with processing mistakes. Participants differed in whether they learned more strongly from negative or positive outcomes, and these differences were reflected in their neural responses. Larger error-related signals were associated with a stronger tendency to learn to avoid previously harmful choices.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Humans differ biologically and cognitively in their sensitivity to mistakes. There is no uniform human response to negative experience.

6. [Learning from Failure Feedback for Subsequent Task Performance: A Matter of Personality?](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1032273)  
   **Katharina Thiel and Thorsten Semrau. Frontiers in Psychology, 2022. 13:1032273. DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1032273.**

   This experiment investigated how personality affects responses to failure feedback. Participants high in conscientiousness or extraversion were more likely to improve their subsequent task performance after failure feedback than participants scoring lower on those traits. The study found no simple universal effect in which failure automatically improved future performance.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Even when people receive the same negative feedback, their responses differ. Human learning from failure depends partly on characteristics of the learner.

7. [Failing to Learn from Failure: How Optimism Impedes Entrepreneurial Innovation](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2020.1359)  
   **Mario Daniele Amore, Orsola Garofalo, and Victor Martin-Sanchez. Organization Science, 2021. 32(4):940-964. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2020.1359.**

   Using firm-level data and a laboratory experiment involving entrepreneurs, the researchers found that dispositional optimism was negatively associated with belief updating following negative feedback. More optimistic entrepreneurs were less likely to revise their expectations, and when they did revise them, the adjustment tended to be smaller.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Persistence and optimism can be useful, but they can also make a person discount evidence that an existing model or strategy is wrong.

## Cognitive Barriers to Correcting Beliefs

8. [Humans Actively Sample Evidence to Support Prior Beliefs](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.71768)  
   **Paula Kaanders, Pradyumna Sepulveda, Tomas Folke, Pietro Ortoleva, and Benedetto De Martino. eLife, 2022. 11:e71768. DOI: 10.7554/eLife.71768.**

   The study shows that confirmation bias can operate before evidence is even interpreted. People actively choose information in ways that favor their existing beliefs. This means that a person can construct an information environment in which contradictory evidence is systematically under-sampled.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Humans do not merely misinterpret history or previous mistakes. We may avoid encountering the evidence that would force us to reconsider our beliefs in the first place.

9. [The Ostrich Problem: Motivated Avoidance or Rejection of Information About Goal Progress](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12071)  
   **Thomas L. Webb, Betty P. I. Chang, and Yael Benn. Social and Personality Psychology Compass, 2013. 7:794-807. DOI: 10.1111/spc3.12071.**

   This review describes situations in which people deliberately avoid information about whether they are making progress toward a goal. Monitoring can reveal unpleasant facts, so people sometimes avoid measurements, records, or other feedback that could contradict the outcome they want to believe.

   **Relevance to the essay:** Better information systems cannot create better decisions if humans refuse to consult them when the information is psychologically uncomfortable.

10. [Biased Belief Priors Versus Biased Belief Updating: Differential Correlates of Depression and Anxiety](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1010176)  
    **Christopher Gagne, Sharon Agai, Christian Ramiro, Peter Dayan, and Sonia Bishop. PLOS Computational Biology, 2022. 18(8):e1010176. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1010176.**

    The paper uses computational models to distinguish beliefs people hold before receiving new evidence from the way they update those beliefs afterward. Its broader significance is that inaccurate beliefs can arise through different mechanisms: biased starting assumptions, biased processing of new evidence, or both.

    **Relevance to the essay:** Simply giving someone correct information may not fix an incorrect worldview because the problem may lie in the mechanism used to incorporate new evidence.

11. [Learned Helplessness at Fifty: Insights from Neuroscience](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000033)  
    **Steven F. Maier and Martin E. P. Seligman. Psychological Review, 2016. 123(4):349-367. DOI: 10.1037/rev0000033.**

    This review revisits fifty years of research on learned helplessness using newer neuroscience evidence. It substantially revises the original interpretation: passivity following uncontrollable negative events is not simply something an organism must "learn." Instead, learning that control is possible appears to engage neural systems that suppress passive responses and support active coping.

    **Relevance to the essay:** Repeated failure can reduce experimentation when people conclude that their actions cannot meaningfully change outcomes. A society can possess information about a problem while individuals cease believing that acting on that information matters.

## Cumulative Culture: Humanity's Existing Intergenerational Memory

12. [Studying Cumulative Cultural Evolution in the Laboratory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607341/)  
    **Christine A. Caldwell and Ailsa E. Millen. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 2008. 363(1509):3529-3539. DOI: 10.1098/rstb.2008.0133. PMID: 18799419; PMCID: PMC2607341.**

    Cumulative cultural evolution describes a process in which innovations are socially transmitted, retained, and subsequently improved. Instead of every generation starting again from zero, useful modifications can persist and accumulate. Laboratory transmission experiments demonstrate that these processes can generate increasingly effective and complex behavior over repeated generations.

    **Relevance to the essay:** Humans already possess something resembling a primitive hive mind. Culture allows one person's discoveries to become another person's starting point.

13. [Ratcheting Up the Ratchet: On the Evolution of Cumulative Culture](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2865079/)  
    **Claudio Tennie, Josep Call, and Michael Tomasello. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 2009. 364(1528):2405-2415. DOI: 10.1098/rstb.2009.0052. PMID: 19620111; PMCID: PMC2865079.**

    The authors distinguish human cumulative culture from forms of cultural transmission observed in other animals. Human culture has a "ratchet effect": successful changes can be preserved so that future generations do not need to rediscover them independently. The authors link this capacity to human social learning, teaching, cooperation, imitation of processes, conformity, and norms.

    **Relevance to the essay:** The statement that every generation must repeat every previous mistake is too strong. Humans are exceptionally capable of inheriting knowledge. The more interesting question is why this ratchet works extremely well for technologies and techniques but appears less reliable for social and political behavior.

## Collective Intelligence

14. [Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1193147)  
    **Anita Williams Woolley, Christopher F. Chabris, Alex Pentland, Nada Hashmi, and Thomas W. Malone. Science, 2010. 330(6004):686-688. DOI: 10.1126/science.1193147. PMID: 20929725.**

    In two studies involving 699 participants working in groups, researchers found evidence for a general collective intelligence factor, called *c*. Groups that performed well on one type of task tended to perform well on other tasks. Collective intelligence was not strongly correlated with either the average intelligence or maximum intelligence of individual members. It was associated with greater social sensitivity and more equal distribution of conversational participation.

    **Relevance to the essay:** A group can display cognitive capabilities that cannot simply be reduced to the intelligence of its smartest individual. Collective intelligence is a measurable phenomenon.

15. [How Social Influence Can Undermine the Wisdom of Crowd Effect](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3107299/)  
    **Jan Lorenz, Heiko Rauhut, Frank Schweitzer, and Dirk Helbing. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2011. 108(22):9020-9025. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1008636108. PMID: 21576485; PMCID: PMC3107299.**

    In an experiment with 144 participants, researchers found that exposing people to other participants' estimates reduced the diversity of opinions without necessarily improving collective accuracy. Social influence could cause estimates to converge while simultaneously increasing participants' confidence. The authors identified effects through which social influence can weaken the wisdom-of-crowds phenomenon.

    **Relevance to the essay:** A hive mind is not automatically intelligent. Connecting people too tightly can eliminate the independent judgments that allow collective intelligence to work. A collective system can therefore amplify shared mistakes as easily as it can aggregate knowledge.

## AI and Collective Intelligence

16. [AI-enhanced Collective Intelligence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10433)  
    **Hao Cui and Taha Yasseri, 2024. arXiv:2403.10433. Preprint/review.**

    The authors examine collective systems containing both humans and artificial intelligence. They argue that humans and AI have complementary capabilities and propose a multilayer network framework for understanding how cognition, information, diversity, and interaction affect collective performance. The paper considers AI not merely as a passive tool but potentially as a participant within a collective intelligence system.

    **Relevance to the essay:** AI creates the possibility of moving beyond collective storage and retrieval toward systems that actively process accumulated human knowledge. This comes closest to the technological interpretation of a "hive mind."

    **Evidence note:** This is an arXiv preprint rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, so it should be treated as an emerging research perspective rather than evidence at the same level as the papers above.

# Synthesis for the Essay

The research suggests that the problem is more complicated than saying that humans simply do not learn from mistakes.

Humans are actually unusually good at transmitting knowledge. Cumulative culture allows discoveries to persist across generations. Writing, education, scientific institutions, libraries, databases, and the Internet can all be viewed as increasingly sophisticated implementations of external collective memory.

The weakness appears elsewhere.

A useful conceptual sequence is:

```text
Experience
    |
    v
Mistake
    |
    v
Recognition
    |
    v
Attention
    |
    v
Interpretation
    |
    v
Belief Update
    |
    v
Behavior Change
    |
    v
Knowledge Transmission
```

Failure can occur at every stage.

A person may not recognize the mistake.

A person may recognize it but disengage because it threatens the ego.

A person may avoid information about the failure.

A person may seek only evidence supporting an existing belief.

A person may receive contradictory evidence but update the underlying belief too little.

A person may understand the lesson but fail to change behavior.

A generation may correctly record the lesson but fail to transmit its significance to the next generation.

The central problem may therefore not be **human memory**.

It may be the gap between:

```text
information -> understanding -> belief revision -> behavior
```

Human civilization has become extraordinarily good at storing information. It is much less obvious that we have become equally good at converting accumulated information into better judgment.

# A More Precise Thesis for "Do We Need Hive Mind?"

The research supports a stronger thesis than "humans are stupid and therefore need a hive mind."

A more defensible formulation is:

> Humans have already created a primitive hive mind through cumulative culture. Knowledge can survive the individuals who discovered it and can accumulate across generations. Yet the existence of collective memory does not guarantee collective learning. Psychological biases interfere with learning from failure, while social influence can cause groups to converge confidently on incorrect beliefs. The next step in collective intelligence may therefore require systems that do more than preserve knowledge. They must help humans recognize relevant historical experience, preserve independent viewpoints, identify contradictions, and convert accumulated evidence into better decisions.

This also introduces an important constraint.

A successful hive mind cannot simply make everyone think alike.

Research on collective intelligence and the wisdom of crowds suggests that diversity and partially independent judgment are valuable. A system that eliminates disagreement may become less intelligent rather than more intelligent.

The design problem is therefore not:

```text
How do we connect every human mind?
```

It is:

```text
How do we preserve individual thought
while allowing humanity to learn
from the accumulated experience of everyone?
```
