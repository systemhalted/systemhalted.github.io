#+TITLE: "It’s Not Illegal" Is Not Enough
#+DESCRIPTION: A neighborhood discussion about e-bikes, parenting, and why following the law does not settle every question about safety and good judgment.
#+DATE: 2026-08-20
#+CATEGORIES: Personal Essays
#+TAGS: parenting, children, safety, responsibility, judgment, community, e-bikes
#+COMMENTS: true
#+TOC: false

A discussion in our neighborhood Facebook group about a child riding an e-bike became an argument about traffic rules. The rider had apparently passed a school bus, and people disagreed about whether the maneuver was legal. Had the bus displayed its stop sign? Was passing allowed on that stretch of road? Had the rider stopped where required?

These are reasonable questions. But as the discussion continued, I felt we were asking a legal question and expecting the answer to settle a much broader concern: whether the child had exercised good judgment.

I was responding to the discussion, not conducting an investigation. I could not establish every detail of what happened from the comments. Still, the argument that passing a vehicle is not necessarily illegal does not establish that a particular attempt to pass was safe. Visibility, oncoming traffic, speed, distance, and the rider’s experience all matter. Something can be permitted and still be a poor decision under the circumstances.

Children need independence. Riding to a friend’s house, exploring the neighborhood, or going fishing without a parent accompanying every trip are valuable experiences. I understand why parents want their children to have that freedom. I also understand why families whose children ride responsibly become frustrated when every e-bike rider gets treated as a problem.

But defending that freedom requires us to take concerns about its use seriously. A complaint about a particular maneuver does not automatically amount to hostility toward everyone who rides an e-bike. We should be able to question a child’s decision without turning the discussion into a contest between people who support e-bikes and people who supposedly hate them.

The same applies to appeals to the law. If "the law is the law" is the basis of an argument, then that standard has to apply consistently. We cannot insist on a precise reading of the rules governing a passing maneuver while dismissing questions about whether the rider and the vehicle meet the applicable requirements. Those questions need verified answers, including an accurate understanding of what kind of vehicle is involved. They should not be settled through guesses about a child’s age or assumptions about what every electric bike can do.

Even after the legal questions are answered, the parenting question remains. Is my child ready to use this vehicle safely around other people?

Personally, I would not get my son an e-bike at his current age. That is my judgment about the responsibility involved and the consequences of a mistake. Other parents may reach a different decision, but I would hope that decision includes more than whether the purchase is allowed or whether other children already have one.

I am nervous enough when my son rides his bicycle or e-scooter around the neighborhood. I want him to have freedom, and I know that freedom involves making decisions without me standing beside him. I also know that children sometimes make poor choices. Mine is no exception. Half the time, I am hoping he does not become the subject of the next community Facebook post.

That possibility should make me more willing to listen if someone raises a concern about him. It does not mean I must accept every accusation. I can ask what happened, examine the evidence, and challenge an unfair characterization. But protecting my child also means helping him recognize when he has made a mistake.

Community discussions have responsibilities on the other side, too. Raising a safety concern should not become an invitation to publicly humiliate a child or pass judgment on an entire family. Once people feel attacked, they tend to defend themselves, and the original concern gets lost. A Facebook argument rarely changes anyone’s mind after that point.

What I want from these conversations is fairly simple. Parents should be able to hear a concern without assuming it is an attack, and neighbors should be able to raise one without making it personal. We should apply rules consistently and acknowledge the limits of what we know.

Most of all, we should teach our children to ask more than whether they are allowed to do something. Before passing a bus, entering an intersection, or accelerating down a neighborhood street, they need to consider what they can see, what they might have missed, and who could be hurt if their judgment is wrong.

"It’s not illegal" may answer one question. As a parent, I need us to ask the others.
